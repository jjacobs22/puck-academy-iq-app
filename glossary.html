📖 Hockey Glossary

🏒 Rink Areas

Boards
Also called: "The wall"
The wooden or fiberglass wall that surrounds the entire ice surface. When someone says "along the boards" or "on the wall," they mean the area right next to this barrier. Players often battle for pucks here.
In context
"Chip it off the boards to your winger."
Half Wall
The area of the boards between the blue line and the goal line, roughly halfway down the zone. It's a key positioning spot in both the offensive and defensive zones—where a lot of puck battles happen during cycles and breakouts.
In context
"Support the half wall on the breakout" means position yourself along the boards, about halfway between the blue line and the goal line, ready to receive a pass.
The Slot
Also called: "The house," "the danger zone"
The high-scoring area directly in front of the net, extending from the crease out to about the faceoff circles. The "high slot" is the area further from the net (near the top of the circles), while the "low slot" is closer to the crease. Defensively, protecting this area is your top priority.
In context
"Don't chase the puck behind the net—hold your position in the slot."
Crease
Also called: "The blue paint"
The blue semi-circle in front of the goal. This is the goalie's protected area. Offensively, you want to establish position at the edge of the crease for tips and rebounds. Defensively, you clear opponents out of here.
The Point
The area near the blue line in the offensive zone where defensemen typically position themselves. When you hear "cover the point," it means take away the opposing defenseman at the blue line.
In context
"I'm rotating back to cover the point" means moving to defend the opposing D-man near the blue line.
Hash Marks
The short lines inside the faceoff circles and along the boards. Players must line up outside these marks during faceoffs until the puck is dropped. They're reference points for positioning.
Behind the Net
Also called: "Below the goal line"
The area between the goal and the end boards. A key area for cycling on offense and puck retrieval on defense. When diagrams show the puck "behind the net," it's in this trapezoid-shaped space.

📍 Positioning & Movement

Gap Control
The distance a defender maintains between themselves and an attacking player. A "tight gap" means staying close to take away time and space. A "loose gap" gives the attacker more room but gives the defender more time to react.
In context
"Close your gap as they cross the blue line" means skate toward the attacker to reduce their time and space.
Inside Position / Getting Inside
Positioning yourself between your opponent and the net (or between your opponent and where they want to go). Having "inside position" means you're closer to the dangerous area than they are. In faceoffs, "getting on someone's inside" means positioning your body between them and where the puck is going.
In context
"Get inside position on their winger" means get your body between that player and your net.
Soft Ice
Also called: "Open ice," "quiet ice"
Areas of the ice with no defenders nearby—open space where you can receive a pass with time. Smart players are always scanning for soft ice.
In context
"Find soft ice in the high slot" means move to an open area where you can receive a pass uncovered.
Net Front Presence
Positioning yourself in front of the opposing goalie to screen their view, deflect shots, or collect rebounds. It's a gritty role—you'll take crosschecks from defenders. But it's where a lot of goals come from.
Backcheck
Skating hard back toward your own zone when the other team has the puck. Good backchecking means picking up your man and applying pressure, not just skating near the play.
In context
"Backcheck hard through the middle" means hustle back while staying in the middle of the ice to cut off passing lanes.
Pinch
When a defenseman moves down from the blue line (into the offensive zone) to keep the puck in or make a play. It's a calculated risk—if you pinch and the puck gets past you, you've given up a potential odd-man rush.
In context
"Don't pinch there—stay home" means don't take the risk of moving down; stay at the blue line.

⚡ Plays & Systems

Cycle / Cycling
An offensive strategy where players move the puck along the boards and behind the net in a circular pattern, maintaining possession while looking for scoring chances. The goal is to tire out defenders and create openings.
In context
"Let's cycle it low" means work the puck around the boards in the offensive zone, usually below the circles.
Breakout
The organized play to move the puck out of your defensive zone. Typically involves specific routes and passing options. Common breakout routes include "up the wall" (along the boards), "rim" (around the boards), and "middle" (through center ice).
In context
"Run the breakout" means execute your team's planned pattern to exit the zone with control.
Forecheck
Pressuring the opposing team in their defensive zone to force turnovers. F1 is the first forechecker (usually the first player in), F2 is the second, and F3 is the third. Different systems have different roles for each.
In context
"You're F1—angle the D" means you're the first in, and your job is to force the defenseman toward the boards.
Odd-Man Rush
Examples: 2-on-1, 3-on-2
When the attacking team has more players than defenders on a rush. A "2-on-1" means two attackers against one defender. These are high-danger situations—defenders need to take away passes and force shots from bad angles.
Rim
Shooting the puck around the boards (the curved glass/wall around the rink) to move it to a teammate or clear the zone. A useful tactic when direct passes are blocked.
In context
"Rim it around" means shoot the puck along the boards to your teammate on the other side.
Backdoor
A play where a player sneaks to the far side of the net (the "weak side") to receive a cross-ice pass for an easy tap-in goal. The defender isn't expecting it because the play appears to be developing on the other side.

⚔️ Faceoffs

Linesman Lean / Ref Position
Before dropping the puck, the linesman's body position can give you a clue about where they'll drop it. If they're leaning toward your forehand side, you may have an easier line to the puck on that side. Watch their shoulders, hips, and where they're looking.
In context
"The ref is leaning your way" means the linesman's body is angled toward you, suggesting the drop may favor your forehand.
Forehand vs. Backhand
Your forehand is the side of your stick blade that faces forward when you hold your stick naturally. Your backhand is the opposite side. On faceoffs, going "forehand" means attacking the puck with your forehand side; going "backhand" means using your backhand. Which to use depends on the situation, your opponent, and the linesman's position.
Tie-Up
A faceoff technique where instead of trying to win the puck cleanly, you use your body and stick to tie up (block) the opposing center, allowing your winger to come in and retrieve the puck.
In context
"Tie him up and let your winger get it" means focus on blocking the opponent, not winning the draw yourself.
Cheat Your Feet
Adjusting your stance in the faceoff circle to give yourself an advantage—often by positioning your feet slightly forward or to one side before the puck drops. This can help you get a quicker start, but if the linesman notices, you might get kicked out of the circle.
Leverage
Using your body position—low center of gravity, strong base, stick pressure—to overpower your opponent on the draw. Good leverage means you're harder to push around and can direct the puck where you want it.
Post-Draw Responsibility
What you do immediately after the faceoff, whether you won or lost it. Winning the draw is only half the battle—you need to know where the puck is going and what your next move is. Lose the draw? Recover and get into defensive position.
